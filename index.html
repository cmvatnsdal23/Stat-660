GDP per Capita vs. Happiness Score (2017–2020)

Using sqldf to merge all four year datasets. Each panel shows one year with a linear trend line and Pearson r value.

gdp_happiness <- sqldf("
  SELECT Country, Score, GDP_per_capita, Year
  FROM happiness_all
  WHERE GDP_per_capita IS NOT NULL AND Score IS NOT NULL
")

r_labels <- gdp_happiness %>%
  group_by(Year) %>%
  summarise(r = round(cor(GDP_per_capita, Score, use = "complete.obs"), 3)) %>%
  mutate(label = paste0("r = ", r), GDP_per_capita = 1.8, Score = 3.2)

Written Analysis

Summary of Findings

The top 10 happiest countries in the most recent year, in order, starting with the happiest, were Finland, Denmark, Iceland, Sweden, the Netherlands, Costa Rica, Norway, Israel, Luxembourg, and Mexico.

Almost every country experienced an overall decrease in happiness between 2017 and 2020, with the exception of Indonesia, which has increased in happiness levels. Japan has stayed relatively steady over time. The United States had an increase in happiness between 2019 and 2020; however, it was not enough to reach the levels in 2017.

Denmark, Finland, Iceland, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, and Switzerland have all been in the top 10 all four years. Canada, Austria, Australia, Luxembourg, and Azerbaijan have all been in the top ten at times, but less consistently than the first 8.

There is a correlation between GDP Per Capita and happiness levels showing a strong positive correlation that has generally weakened through time. The R value slightly increased from 0.809 to 0.811 and then fell to 0.800, and then again to 0.771 in 2020. Africa has the lowest median happiness while Europe has the highest median happiness. North and South America have a lower variation in happiness. Europe and Asia have large variations in happiness levels. North America and Asia both have large outliers that are significantly less happy than the other countries.

Looking at the bubble chart, in 2020, while both social support and life expectancy positively impact happiness, life expectancy appears to have a larger impact on happiness. The circles in the top right that represent countries with high life expectancy and high GDP per capita are almost exclusively happy; the lower circles on the right are larger than the higher circles on the left of the chart.
